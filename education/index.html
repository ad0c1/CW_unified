The signal, the carrier, and the residual

Why a group's minority-voice cycles are not measurement noise — and how one weighting scheme catches them across personality tests, forced-choice perception, and brain networks.

Yong Wook Shin, University of Ulsan / Asan Medical Center 2026-07-12 Draft · v0.1

Almost every graph we compute from group data — a personality-item network, a paired-comparison ranking, a resting-state brain correlation matrix — is built by pooling across subjects. Take the average correlation, or the majority preference, and treat that as the group's edge. This move is quiet, universal, and usually harmless.

But it hides two things at once. It hides which subjects actually carry the edge — is a strong correlation supported by everyone, or by an idiosyncratic minority? And it flattens what remains after the dominant story is subtracted — the residual — into what most papers call error.

The claim of this line of work is simple: after weighting each edge by the coherence of its supporting group, the residual that remains is not noise. It is structured, reproducible across resamples, and correlates with real outcomes. Something is there.

1Two ideas, one framework

Two moves make everything else in this project possible. Neither is new alone — the novelty is applying them together, and using the second to diagnose the first.

Move 1 — carrier weighting

An edge weight in a subject-averaged network is really two numbers glued together: how large the mean signal is, and how uniformly subjects contribute to it. Carrier weighting separates them. For a symmetric similarity edge (like an item correlation), the weight becomes

w*ij = |ρij| · (1 − Giniij) · max(Ōij, 0)

Three factors, each a different question about the same edge. Read them left to right.

Factor 1 · |ρij| — the raw signal

The absolute value of the correlation between items i and j, computed on the group. For binary/ordinal item responses we use the tetrachoric (or polychoric) estimator, not Pearson, so that a 0/1 dichotomy does not attenuate the coefficient. This factor is what a naïve pooled network uses and stops there. It answers only: on average, how tightly do these two items co-vary? It does not ask who was co-varying. The next two factors do.

Factor 2 · (1 − Giniij) — dispersion of the carrier

The carrier of an edge is the subject-by-subject vector of centered products Sij(s) = (xi,s − x̄i)(xj,s − x̄j). This is the atomic contribution each respondent makes to that particular edge's covariance. Some subjects contribute a lot, some contribute nothing, some contribute in the opposite direction. Averaging S across subjects gives you the covariance. But averaging hides how the average was made.

The Gini coefficient of |S| measures inequality among the contributors — the same tool an economist uses on incomes. Gini = 0 means every subject contributes exactly the same magnitude; Gini = 1 means one subject contributes everything and the rest contribute nothing.

Gini(|Sij|) → 0   carrier is broad — everyone chips in equally   ⟹ (1 − Gini) ≈ 1   keep the edge
Gini(|Sij|) → 1   carrier is concentrated — one clique carries it   ⟹ (1 − Gini) ≈ 0   shrink the edge

Why this is the right thing to punish: a covariance sustained by a small handful of respondents is precisely the kind of edge that will not replicate in a fresh sample, because the underlying subgroup is not guaranteed to reappear. Molenaar's ergodicity–non-ergodicity gap is the formal name — the group-level statistic (the correlation) fails to describe most of the individuals it is computed from. The 1 − Gini multiplier is a direct penalty for that gap: broadly-carried edges keep their signal, narrowly-carried edges lose it.

If every subject contributes equally, the ranks average out and the two terms cancel to zero. If one subject carries everything, the last term dominates and Gini approaches (n − 1) / n.

Our carrier sits three-quarters of the way from equality to max concentration on n = 8 — dominated by subject H's contribution of 4, which is 44% of the total from a single respondent out of eight.

The hypothetical broad S column is not derived from a second set of subject responses. It is a counterfactual carrier vector with the same total (sum = 9) and therefore the same mean covariance of 1.125, but with the contributions spread almost evenly across all eight subjects. It answers the question: "if these eight subjects had produced the same covariance without any single dominator, what would that carrier look like?" Its Gini lives in a completely different world:

Gini(|alt S|) ≈ 0.097   ⟹   1 − Gini ≈ 0.903  // broad carrier

Same raw covariance. Same tetrachoric ρ would come out equal after standardisation. But the CW factor differs by 2.4× — the first edge would shrink to roughly 40% of its raw weight, while the second would keep 90%. That gap is what Pearson-thresholded networks throw away when they treat both edges as identical, and it is exactly what Gini catches. In the actual eight-subject data above, subject H is a single respondent whose absence would flatten the correlation entirely; treating that edge as a robust group property would be a replication trap. The 1 − Gini factor sees the trap before the walktrap does.

Factor 3 · max(Ōij, 0) — neighbourhood coherence

The last factor asks a subtler question. Not how many subjects carry this edge, but whether the same subjects also carry the surrounding edges. For every edge e' that shares a node with e = (i,j), compute the cosine similarity between the two carrier vectors Se and Se'. Then Ōij is the mean of those cosines.

Ōij = meane' shares a node with (i,j)   cos( Sij, Se' )

Ō ≈ 1 means the respondents who carry this edge are also the respondents who carry the neighbouring edges: a coherent local module of subjects supports a coherent local module of edges. Ō ≈ 0 means this edge is carried by a different crowd than its neighbours — it may still be strong on average, but it is orthogonal to the module it sits inside, and the walktrap community structure will not know what to do with it.

The max(Ō, 0) clip is deliberate. A negative Ō means this edge's carrier vector points opposite to its neighbourhood — that is, the same respondents who carry the local module actively push against this edge. Rather than let that fight negatively-weight the edge into the graph (which would corrupt walktrap on |w*|), we hard-zero it. If your edge is anti-coherent with its neighbourhood, it does not belong in this planar module at all.

A three-item toy for Ō

Extend the eight-subject example by adding a third item k. There are now three edges — (i, j), (i, k), (j, k) — and the edge we focus on, e = (i, j), has two neighbours that share a node with it: (i, k) shares node i, and (j, k) shares node j. Ōij is the mean of two cosines.

We try two versions of item k. In version A the responses on k co-vary with i and j (a coherent local module). In version B the responses on k move in the opposite direction from i and j (an anti-coherent, cross-axis item). All three item means come out to 3.0 either way, so d = x − 3.

The three carrier vectors all point roughly in the same direction; their cosines land around +0.87, and Ō stays positive and large. The edge (i, j) is embedded inside a coherent little module, and the factor keeps 87% of its weight.

In version B, the same subjects who carry (i, j) push in the opposite direction on the (i, k) and (j, k) edges — subject H, who piled a +4 contribution on (i, j), now contributes −4 to both neighbouring edges. The two cosines flip to around −0.94, Ō is deeply negative, and the max(·, 0) clip zeroes the edge entirely. The (i, j) edge might still be strong on the group correlation matrix (ρ was 0.82), but it does not belong in the same local module as its neighbours — it is a cross-axis edge, and Factor 3 refuses to let it join the walktrap community that (i, k) and (j, k) form.

This is exactly the mechanism that catches "borderline" items in the GW-21 short form: q40 sits at 12.5% cross-axis edge share in the network — barely under the 20% Stage-1 cutoff — precisely because its Ō is small but not negative. Items with negative Ō get zeroed out by Factor 3 before Stage-1 even sees them.

The three factors together

Multiplying gives an edge weight that fires only when all three conditions are met: the raw signal is real (|ρ| is not near zero), the carrier is broad (Gini is small), and the carrier is coherent with its neighbourhood (Ō is positive). Any one of the three going to zero zeros the edge. This is a strict AND, not a soft weighted sum — the strictness is what gives CW-TMFG its selectivity.

Row A is the case where a classical Pearson-thresholded network and CW-TMFG agree; rows B and C are the cases where they diverge — and where CW-TMFG earns its keep by not being fooled.

The directed-edge cousin

For a directed preference edge (i chosen over j), the same idea takes a simpler form because we have discrete win/lose choices instead of continuous covariances:

cij = |2 Pij − 1|   // carrier = decisiveness of the majority
w*i→j = cij · mij   // carrier × signed mean intensity

Here the carrier cij collapses the Gini + Ō machinery into one number, because a directed edge has only two states (i beats j, or j beats i). The carrier is 0 when the group is split 50-50 and 1 when the group is unanimous. Factor 3 (neighbourhood coherence) is not needed at the edge level — instead, its work reappears in the Hodge curl component after decomposition, which is exactly the topic of Move 2.

Move 2 — Hodge decomposition

Once the edges are weighted, we ask: what is this flow really made of? For directed edges — preferences, transitions, comparisons — the classical answer from combinatorial Hodge theory is that any edge flow decomposes uniquely into a gradient (a global ranking, transitive) and a curl (cyclic residual on triangles), with a harmonic piece that vanishes on complete graphs.

w*  =  grad(s) + xcurl + xharm

The gradient is what a psychometrician would call the reliable dimension: a scale on the nodes. The curl is what many methods papers treat as noise: irreducible three-cycles like A beats B beats C beats A. A curl that survives carrier weighting is evidence of attenuation-resistant cyclic structure, but survival alone is not a sampling test. A respondent-level bootstrap or a carrier-destroying permutation null is still required before calling the residual more than sampling variation.

4What we found last week

The prediction locked at the start of the project was blunt: after applying carrier weighting to a real dataset of forced-choice preferences, the cyclic residual should still be non-zero. If it collapsed under CW, the whole "structured collective residual" story would fail its first real test.

The dataset was 145 respondents making forced-choice preferences among 8 word stimuli — a bootstrap file from an ongoing intransitivity project. Each subject's antisymmetric win matrix was aggregated into two group-level matrices P (majority proportions) and M (mean signed intensity). The CW flow was decomposed on the complete graph K8.

How each number is obtained

First orient each of the 28 unique pairs as i → j with i < j. For respondent k, encode the observed choice as x(k)ij = +1 when i wins and −1 when j wins (or retain its signed intensity when the source contains one). Then compute, edge by edge:

Pij = (1/N) Σk 1[x(k)ij > 0]   ;   Mij = (1/N) Σk x(k)ij
cij = |2Pij − 1|   ;   Wcw,ij = cij Mij

The unweighted comparison sets cij = 1, so Wuniform,ij = Mij. For strictly binary ±1 choices, Mij = 2Pij − 1; consequently Wcw,ij = sign(Mij)|Mij|2. This square is not an extra fitted parameter: it follows because majority strength and mean signed choice contain the same information in binary data. If the source uses graded signed intensity, P and M remain distinct.

Stack the 28 values of Wcw,ij into one edge vector w. Let A be the 28 × 8 oriented incidence matrix: the row for edge i → j contains +1 at node i, −1 at node j, and zero elsewhere. The least-squares ranking scores, transitive flow, and cyclic residual are:

ŝ = (ATA)†ATw   ;   grad = Aŝ   ;   curl = w − grad

Finally, calculate every magnitude in the same 28-edge space, ∥z∥E = √Σi<jzij2. Orthogonality gives a compulsory check: ∥w∥E2 = ∥grad∥E2 + ∥curl∥E2. The original output failed this check because its curl used the full-matrix norm.

Audit correction, 13 July 2026. The original table mixed two norm conventions: Wcw and its gradient were measured on the 28 unique oriented edges, whereas the curl was measured on the full 8 × 8 antisymmetric matrix. A full-matrix Frobenius norm counts each edge twice, so its value is √2 times the edge-vector norm. Dividing both reported cyclicity ratios by √2 gives ηcw ≈ 0.189 and ηuniform ≈ 0.236. The ratio 0.189/0.236 ≈ 0.80 is therefore preserved, but its correct reading is that about 80% of the normalized cyclicity ratio remains after weighting — not that 80% of the raw curl magnitude survives.

The concentration of the largest triangle circulations around node 5 is a useful localization result, but it does not by itself rule out sampling variation. That claim requires a respondent-level bootstrap confidence interval or a permutation/null distribution computed with the complete CW pipeline inside every resample. Until that test is run on the recoverable 145-person source cohort, this result is descriptive evidence of a non-zero, localized residual rather than a sampling-error test.

Numerical note. The rounded legacy values still miss the exact orthogonal identity slightly: √(2.134² + (0.581/√2)²) ≈ 2.173 rather than 2.171. The corrected values above repair the known √2 unit error, but the final three-decimal estimates must come from a clean replay using one edge-vector norm throughout.

The carrier is a filter. What passes through is not the whole graph. What passes through, and stays cyclic, is the group's collective disagreement with itself — and it deserves an explanation, not a footnote.

5Where this is going

The immediate next steps are the same across the three fields, and the education page will grow along with them: extend the carrier machinery to symmetric similarity edges (already done for personality), replicate the intransitivity result across other stimulus modalities (colour, drawings, virtual faces), and repeat the whole pipeline on ABIDE brain functional connectivity with hemispheric side as the natural dominant partition and cross-hemispheric edges as the residual. If the pattern holds up in the brain data, the unified paper writes itself — and the reader of this page will already know how to read it.
